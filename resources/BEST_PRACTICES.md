# Best-Practice Guidelines for a Job Application

## 1. Purpose

A strong job application is concise, role-specific, and high-signal.

Recruiters typically spend 30–60 seconds scanning. The application must make the candidate’s relevance clear immediately.

The CV proves qualification.
The application proves relevance.

The application is not a compressed CV. It should answer:

> Why is this candidate useful for this specific role, in this specific company, right now?

## 2. Core Principles

Default to less. Add only what strengthens the case.

Prioritize:

* Relevance over impressiveness
* Clarity over completeness
* Evidence over explanation
* Specificity over breadth
* Fast scanning over full context

Avoid:

* Repeating the CV
* Summarizing career history
* Trying to show the full profile
* Including strong but unrelated achievements
* Using generic motivation or positioning

Rule:

> A smaller, highly relevant example beats a stronger but unrelated one.

## 3. Target Length

Target:

* ½ page
* 200–250 words
* 3–4 short paragraphs

Absolute maximum:

* ¾ page
* 250 words
* 4 paragraphs, excluding headline and sign-off

If the draft is longer, cut.

## 4. Mandatory Pre-Writing Step

Before writing, silently decide:

1. What is the employer really hiring this person to solve?
2. What are the 2–3 most important needs in the job post?
3. Which 1–2 candidate strengths best match those needs?
4. Which CV details are irrelevant and must be excluded?

Then write only from those decisions.

Do not:

* Represent the full CV
* Balance across all past roles
* Include achievements simply because they are impressive
* Add details that do not directly support the role-specific argument

## 5. Mandatory Relevance Filter

Every sentence must pass this test:

1. Does this sentence respond to a concrete need in the job post?
2. Does it explain why the candidate is useful to this employer now?
3. Would the application be weaker without it?

If any answer is no, remove the sentence.

The application should make a narrow, tailored argument:

> This is the specific problem I can help you solve, and here is the clearest proof.

## 6. CV Use Rule: Translate, Do Not Transfer

The CV is source material, not content to reuse.

Before writing:

1. Extract the 2–3 most important needs from the job post.
2. Select only the CV evidence that directly proves the candidate can meet those needs.
3. Ignore all other achievements, even if impressive.

Do not include the following unless the detail directly strengthens the argument for this role:

* Team size
* Budget size
* Years of experience
* Lists of technologies
* Lists of regulations
* Generic leadership responsibilities
* Career history
* Multiple past roles
* Internal organisational details
* Platform names
* Project names
* Delivery metrics

A CV fact may only be used if it passes this test:

> This matters for the employer because…

If the answer is weak, generic, or only impressive in isolation, remove it.

Rule:

> A CV detail must be transformed from “what I did” into “why this matters here.”

## 7. Application vs CV

Do not answer:

> What has the candidate done?

Answer:

> Why is this candidate the right answer to this employer’s current problem?

Do not:

* Describe responsibilities
* Walk through past roles
* Restate CV facts
* Use metrics only because they are impressive

Only include:

* Translated impact
* Contextualized experience
* Evidence that directly supports the role-specific argument

Examples:

Bad:

> I led 20+ solution architects across four domains and chaired an Architecture Council.

Better:

> I have created architecture governance that helped complex delivery organisations make consistent decisions across domains.

Bad:

> I lead three engineering teams with responsibility for four critical platforms and DKK 20m+ budgets.

Better:

> I know how to modernise critical platforms while keeping delivery, operations, and governance aligned.

## 8. Required Structure

Use this structure unless there is a strong reason not to.

### 8.1 Headline

Mandatory.

Maximum 1 line.

Must communicate:

* Role alignment
* Core value

Keep it specific and concrete.

Avoid:

* Generic titles
* Complex phrasing
* Broad positioning

Examples:

* Enterprise Architecture Leader | Turning regulated complexity into executable technology direction
* AI & Data Leader | Building scalable, business-aligned data capabilities
* Engineering Leader | Modernising critical platforms without slowing delivery

Rule:

> The reader should understand the candidate’s fit before reading the first paragraph.

### 8.2 Opening

Length:

* 1–2 lines

Start with the most relevant signal for this role.

Choose one:

* A concrete problem the candidate has solved that matches the employer’s situation
* A clear scope or ownership that mirrors the employer’s need
* A measurable result only if it directly fits the role context

The opening must feel natural in the company’s context.

Avoid:

* Generic positioning
* Broad self-description
* Forced metrics
* “I am excited to apply”
* Same opening across applications

Rule:

> If the opening could be reused unchanged across applications, it fails.

### 8.3 Role Alignment

Length:

* 2–3 lines

Show understanding of the specific challenge in the job post.

Be concrete and direct.

Do not:

* Over-explain the company’s situation
* Flatter the company
* Restate the job ad
* Add generic motivation

### 8.4 Proof

This is the core section.

Maximum:

* 2 examples
* 1–2 lines per example

Each example must connect directly to a specific need in the job post.

Focus on:

* What changed
* Why it matters for this role
* The candidate’s relevant contribution

Do not:

* Describe responsibilities
* Summarize previous roles
* Include unrelated achievements
* Add extra examples for coverage

### 8.5 Fit

Optional.

Length:

* 1–2 lines

Only include if it adds something new.

Use this section for:

* Specific motivation
* Relevant working style
* Company-context fit

Do not include generic enthusiasm.

### 8.6 Closing

Length:

* 1–2 lines

Keep it simple and direct.

State interest in discussing the role.

Do not repeat the argument.

## 9. Alternative Opening: Context-First Option

Use this when the role is problem-heavy, transformation-oriented, or senior.

Structure:

* Line 1: State the problem space in the employer’s context
* Line 2: Anchor with the candidate’s relevant experience

Example:

> Complex technology environments need architecture leadership that can turn strategy into practical decisions across teams, platforms, and governance. That is where my experience is strongest: creating direction, alignment, and delivery momentum in regulated organisations.

This often reads more naturally than starting with a metric.

## 10. Content Limits

Maximum:

* 1 central argument
* 2–3 core messages
* 2 supporting examples
* 4 paragraphs
* 250 words

Avoid:

* Long lists of capabilities
* Repeated themes across paragraphs
* Multiple examples from the same angle
* Career chronology
* Broad summaries of experience
* Generic leadership claims
* Safe but diluted positioning

Rule:

> If you mention something, it must be one of the strongest role-specific signals.

## 11. No Capability Lists

Avoid lists of:

* Domains
* Technologies
* Regulations
* Platforms
* Responsibilities
* Leadership traits

A list is allowed only if:

* Every item is explicitly central to the job post
* The sentence would be weaker without the list

Otherwise, replace the list with one sharper point.

Bad:

> I bring experience across cloud, data, integration, security, governance, compliance, and strategic programs.

Better:

> I bring the ability to turn enterprise architecture into practical decisions that teams can execute in a regulated environment.

Bad:

> I have worked with GDPR, DORA, NIS2, cloud, data platforms, ETL/ELT, medallion architecture, and enterprise reporting.

Better:

> I have led architecture and delivery decisions where compliance, resilience, and modernisation had to move together.

## 12. Reusability Filter

Any sentence reused from a previous application must be challenged.

If it is not tailored to the role, rewrite or remove it.

Company names alone do not make a sentence tailored.

A tailored sentence must reflect:

* The company’s situation
* The role’s specific challenge
* The candidate’s relevant value

Rule:

> If a sentence could be reused unchanged across applications, it fails.

## 13. Readability and Density

Use:

* Short sentences
* Short paragraphs
* One main idea per sentence
* Plain wording
* Direct phrasing

Avoid:

* Dense paragraphs
* Stacked concepts
* Multi-clause sentences
* Inserted explanations mid-sentence
* Over-explaining relevance

Paragraph limit:

* Maximum 2–3 lines per paragraph

Density test:

> If a paragraph requires rereading, it fails.

## 14. Sentence Structure

Do not use:

* Em dashes
* Semicolons
* Complex punctuation
* Long nested sentences

Prefer:

* Short, direct sentences
* One main idea per sentence
* Clear subject and verb
* Simple punctuation

Rule:

> If a sentence cannot be read once without effort, rewrite it.

## 15. Motivation

Maximum:

* 2 lines

Motivation must be specific to the role and company context.

Avoid generic phrasing such as:

* “I am excited about the opportunity”
* “Your company’s mission appeals to me”
* “I would like to contribute to your continued success”
* “I am motivated by working with talented people”

Only include motivation if it strengthens the case.

Bad:

> The opportunity to work for your respected company is very exciting.

Better:

> I am drawn to this role because the architecture decisions sit close to business-critical execution, where governance must create speed rather than distance.

## 16. Tone and Style

Use a tone that is:

* Direct
* Factual
* Confident
* Calm
* Specific

Avoid:

* Buzzwords
* Over-explaining
* Soft language
* Inflated claims
* Generic enthusiasm
* Corporate clichés

## 17. Formatting Rules

Use plain and consistent formatting.

Do not use:

* Bold formatting
* Italics for emphasis
* Visual styling to highlight content
* Decorative separators

Let structure and wording carry emphasis.

## 18. Sign-Off

Use a simple closing.

Example:

Best regards
Lars Mikki Poulsen

Do not include:

* Phone number
* Email address
* Address
* LinkedIn
* Other contact details

These belong in the CV or application system.

## 19. Mandatory Cut Rule

After writing the first draft:

1. Remove at least 15%.
2. Review again.
3. Remove anything non-essential.

Cut especially:

* Generic motivation
* Repeated leadership claims
* Lists
* CV facts without clear role relevance
* Metrics that do not directly match the job post
* Sentences that explain instead of proving
* Any detail that shows seniority but not relevance

Rule:

> If removal does not weaken the argument, the sentence should not be there.

## 20. Common Failure Patterns

Avoid:

* Too much text
* Dense paragraphs
* Over-explaining relevance
* Trying to show the full profile
* Generic opening
* Reusing the same opening across applications
* Repeating CV content
* Listing technologies or domains
* Listing regulations
* Including impressive but unrelated metrics
* Writing a career summary
* Safe but diluted positioning
* Motivation that could apply to any company

## 21. Final Quality Check

Before sending, check:

* Is it 250 words or less?
* Is the opening relevant and natural for this role?
* Is the opening impossible to reuse unchanged for another application?
* Is there one clear central argument?
* Are there maximum 2 examples?
* Does every example directly match the job post?
* Does it avoid repeating the CV?
* Does it avoid capability lists?
* Does it exclude irrelevant metrics?
* Can anything be removed without weakening the case?
* Can it be understood in under 30 seconds?

If yes, ready.
